Measuring Enterprise AI Coding Productivity: Beyond Adoption Rate

The real challenge is not proving that AI makes people faster. It is proving what that speed changes in the organization.
Johnny Xiaoliang Li (李晓亮) September 2026
English 中文版本 ↓

When management asks, “How are we doing with AI?”, the question is usually not really about usage.

At the beginning, people ask how many developers are using AI, what the acceptance rate looks like, or how much code AI generates. Those numbers are easy to get.

The difficult question comes immediately after:

So what changed?

If an engineer becomes 10% or 20% faster, what does that mean for the company? Do projects finish earlier? Can the same team deliver more? Can we reduce external resources? Can 50 people become 40?

This is where AI productivity becomes much harder than AI adoption.

A good-looking AI metric can still hide a weak result

I saw this clearly when looking at coding-assistant data.

The suggestion acceptance rate was around one-third. My first reaction was: that is not bad. Developers were clearly finding the tool useful.

But when we looked at how much AI-generated code actually entered the measured code output, the number was only in the low single digits.

That was much less exciting.

Both statements were true:

The tool was useful.

But it had not yet changed the production model very much.

That distinction matters.

A reasonable acceptance rate does not mean large organizational impact. High usage does not mean high productivity.

The question I care about is:

How much of that AI usage survives all the way to business value?

Individual productivity is not organizational productivity

Suppose an engineer really saves 20% of coding time.

A business leader will naturally ask:

If everyone is 20% faster, why don't we need 20% fewer people?

Mathematically, the question sounds reasonable. In a real organization, it is much more complicated.

Saved time does not automatically become removable headcount. It may become more iterations, more requirements, more testing, more support work, or simply more work being completed.

There is also a conversion cost. People need time to learn how to work with AI. Existing workflows need to change. Legacy systems need context. Responsibilities have to change before capacity can really be released.

So I now separate two ideas:

Productivity gain

and

Productivity realization

AI may create the first one quickly. The second one can take much longer.

This is also the difficult part to explain upward. Management does not ultimately want an AI metric. Management wants time, cost, capacity and eventually people.

I would measure the workflow, not just the AI tool

Most AI dashboards tell us what happened inside the AI tool. I would go further.

I want to know whether software development itself has changed.

Has PR cycle time improved? Has requirement-to-production time become shorter? Can one engineer take a requirement further from start to finish? Has rework decreased? Has quality improved?

These questions matter more than counting generated code.

AI can make people busy faster without making the organization productive faster.

A developer may modify the same feature five times in half the time. The dashboard sees more commits and more activity. But the business may receive no additional value.

One person completing a project is not the end of the story

AI can allow a strong engineer to do work that previously required several people. That is real productivity.

But I would still ask one more question:

Who maintains it afterwards?

If only one person really understands the project, the company may have gained delivery speed but created a new dependency.

When someone else takes over, that person may need to spend significant time reconstructing the context, understanding the code, or asking AI to rebuild the reasoning again.

Sometimes we save time during creation and pay it back during maintenance.

So local productivity can improve while organizational maintainability gets worse.

I don't believe headcount reduction can be extrapolated linearly

There is a popular idea that AI will allow a very small team to replace a much larger one.

For some work, yes. But if 50 people become 10, those 10 people are not simply doing more coding.

They need broader business knowledge, stronger engineering judgment, more ownership and much more self-management.

And then there is another question:

If someone can really do the work of several people with AI, why would that person remain easy to retain in a traditional organization?

AI increases productivity. But it may also increase the market value and independence of the most capable people.

So “fewer people” is not only a technology question. It is also an organizational and talent question.

What I actually want to measure

AI Usage → Workflow Change → Engineering Outcome → Enterprise Realization

The common mistake is jumping from the first box directly to the last one.

High AI usage does not automatically mean fewer people. Faster coding does not automatically mean faster delivery. More code does not automatically mean more value. Individual productivity does not automatically become organizational productivity.

That conversion is the real work.

The question I care about now

I am less interested in asking:

How much AI-generated code do we have?

I am more interested in asking:

Where did the productivity gain go?

Did it reduce cycle time? Did it release capacity? Did it reduce dependency? Did it improve quality? Did it change how the team works?

And eventually, did the organization get something measurable back?

For me, that is the difference between using AI and transforming with AI.

中文版本

企业AI研发效能：为什么“大家都在用”还远远不够

管理层问“AI用得怎么样”的时候，真正想问的其实通常不是： 有多少人在用、采纳率是多少、生成了多少代码。

这些数字并不难拿。

真正难的是下一句：

然后呢？组织到底发生了什么变化？

如果一个研发人员因为AI快了10%、20%，甚至更多， 那么公司最后得到了什么？

项目是不是更早上线了？ 同样的人是不是可以做更多事情？ 外包是不是可以少一点？ 50个人是不是可以变成40个人？

到这里，AI提效就不再是一个工具使用率的问题了。

一个看起来不错的数字，可能同时藏着一个不太好看的结果

我自己看研发AI数据的时候，就遇到过这种情况。

按次计算的AI建议采纳率大约在三成左右。 我第一反应其实是：这个数字还可以。

说明开发人员不是为了完成任务去打开AI， 而是真的觉得有一些建议可以用。

但再往下看，真正进入整体代码产出的AI代码比例却只有很低的个位数。

那我的感觉就完全不一样了。

两个结论可以同时成立：

工具是有用的。

但它还远远没有改变研发的生产方式。

所以我现在看到一个很高的AI使用率，或者一个看起来不错的采纳率， 第一反应已经不会是“AI提效成功了”。

我会继续问：

这些AI使用，最后有多少真的转化成了组织价值？

个人快了，不等于组织就可以同比例少人

假设一个研发人员真的因为AI节省了20%的编码时间。

老板一定会继续问：

如果每个人都快20%，为什么人数不能少20%？

这个问题从数学上没有问题。 但是放进一个真实组织里面，就不是这么简单。

省出来的时间可能变成更多需求、更多迭代、更多测试、 更多支持工作，也可能只是把以前来不及做的事情做掉。

中间还有一个转化周期。

人要学习怎么真正把AI用好； 原来的研发流程要变化； 老系统需要重新建立上下文； 团队的职责和分工也要跟着调整。

所以我现在会把两件事情分开：

提效发生了没有？

和

提效兑现了没有？

AI可能很快就让个人变快。

但要真正变成公司的时间、产能、成本，甚至人力变化， 中间还有很长一段路。

如果今天重新搭这套体系，我更想看研发过程到底有没有变化

现在很多AI看板，本质上还是在看AI工具自己产生的数据。

使用率、采纳率、生成代码量，这些当然要看。

但我更想知道：

PR提交是不是变快了？ 一个需求从开始到上线是不是缩短了？ 一个人是不是可以承担更完整的端到端工作？ 重复修改是不是少了？ 质量是不是更好了？

因为还有另外一种可能：

AI让一个人每一次修改都非常快， 但是同样一个东西反复改了五遍。

看板会告诉你： Commit多了，代码多了，动作更多了。

但业务价值未必增加。

AI可以让人“忙得更快”，但不一定让组织“产出得更快”。

一个人把项目做出来，也不是故事的结束

AI现在确实可能让一个能力比较强的人， 独立完成以前需要几个人协同才能做出来的项目。

这当然是真实的效率提升。

但我还是会继续问一句：

然后谁来维护？

如果最后只有这个人真正理解整个系统， 那公司在交付阶段省下来的时间， 可能在后面的维护、交接和知识重建阶段重新付出去。

别的人接手以后，要重新熟悉代码、重新理解上下文， 有时候甚至还要重新花大量Token让AI把原来的逻辑再解释一遍。

所以我越来越关注一个问题：

局部效率提高以后，组织的可维护性是不是反而下降了？

我也不相信“50个人直接变10个人”这种线性推导

AI当然会减少一部分工作的用人量，这件事情我并不怀疑。

但如果50个人真的变成10个人， 那剩下的10个人绝对不是简单地“多写五倍代码”。

他们需要更懂业务、更懂技术、更有判断力， 能管更大的范围，也要有更强的自我驱动力。

然后又会出现另外一个问题：

如果一个人真的可以借助AI承担原来几个人的价值， 这种人为什么还会那么容易留在传统企业里面？

AI放大的是个人生产力。

但它同时也可能放大优秀人才的市场价值和独立性。

所以最后“少多少人”，并不是一个简单的AI技术问题， 它还是组织模式和人才问题。

我现在真正想看的，是这条转化链

AI使用 → 工作方式变化 → 研发结果变化 → 企业价值兑现

最大的问题，是很多公司从第一格直接跳到了最后一格。

使用率很高，不等于人可以马上减少。

编码变快，不等于项目一定更快。

代码更多，不等于价值更多。

个人效率提高，也不会自动变成组织效率提高。

真正困难的，就是中间这段转化。

所以我现在最关心的问题变了

我已经不太关心单纯问：

AI写了多少代码？

我更想问：

提升出来的效率，最后到底去了哪里？

它有没有变成更短的周期？

有没有释放真正的产能？

有没有减少重复劳动和人员依赖？

有没有提高质量？

有没有真正改变团队的工作方式？

最后，公司有没有得到可以算得出来的东西？

对我来说，这就是 “用了AI” 和 “被AI改变了” 之间的区别。
